CHAPTER 10Part III · Reasoning & Training

Inference-Time Compute

The new scaling axis. Since Kaplan and Chinchilla, "more compute = better model" meant training. In 2024-2025 a second axis opened: thinking longer at inference. We unpack when it works and when it plateaus.

2020 Kaplan scaling laws 2022 Chinchilla 2022 Self-consistency 2023 Tree of Thoughts 2024-08 Snell et al. — inference scaling laws 2024-09 o1 · "think longer" 2025-2026 production reasoning models
🧒 ELI5 — explain like I'm five

A student can get better at a test in two ways: (1) study more before the test ("train longer"), or (2) spend more time thinking per question on the test ("think longer"). For years we only knew how to scale (1). 2024 was the year we got scaling laws for (2).

Turns out, for hard problems, ten minutes of thinking on a small model can beat thirty seconds of thinking on a big model. That changes the economics of AI.

10.1 The Axis Shift

Training a frontier model takes months and billions of dollars. Deploying it answers questions in 5 seconds. The implicit assumption has been: deployment cost is fixed and small.

Reasoning models broke that. An o1-style "extended thinking" run can spend 10⁴–10⁵ tokens of hidden chain-of-thought before a single user-visible word. The inference side of the budget is no longer invisible.

Typical accuracy-vs-think-tokens curve. Sharp rise through CoT and search; diminishing returns beyond.

10.2 Methods

  • Chain-of-thought (CoT). The cheapest form — just let the model think out loud before answering.
  • Self-consistency. Sample N chains, majority-vote the answer. Works when the answer space is small.
  • Best-of-N with verifier. Sample N answers, a verifier (unit test, grader, PRM) picks the best.
  • Tree search (ToT, LATS, MCTS-over-thoughts). Explore a tree of partial reasoning; prune; expand promising branches.
  • Process-reward-guided search. PRM scores per step; use it as a heuristic in the search.

10.3 The Curves — Diminishing Returns

Snell et al. (2024) fit empirical curves for "given a compute budget B at inference, optimal accuracy A(B)." Key findings:

  • There's a sweet spot: past some multiple of the base-model compute, additional inference tokens are less cost-effective than using a larger base model.
  • The sweet spot depends on task difficulty. Hard problems can absorb more inference compute.
  • Verifier-guided methods (PRM + tree search) outperform naive best-of-N at the same budget.

10.4 When Small + Long Thinking Beats Big + Short

The big economic story of 2025: on well-specified tasks (math, coding, structured extraction), a small reasoning model with inference compute can match a much larger one-shot model at lower total cost. Two consequences:

  • Hosting-providers offer "thinking budget" sliders; customers pick per-query tradeoffs.
  • Routers (Ch 14) now choose not just which model but how hard it should think.
New scaling law

Inference compute compounds with RL training. o1 → o3 came not from more pretraining but from better RLVR + more inference compute. This is the next ~3-5 years of capability.

Task-specific hack

Inference scaling helps on verifiable tasks and plateaus elsewhere. On open-ended creative work, "thinking longer" barely moves quality. Don't mistake a benchmark win for a paradigm shift.

❓ Open questions

  • Is there an inference-compute scaling law analog of Chinchilla for training?
  • How much of inference-compute gain is training-compute in disguise (the RL that enabled the reasoning)?
  • Can we train a model to adaptively decide when to think vs. answer directly, per query?
