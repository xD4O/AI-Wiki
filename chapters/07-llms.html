CHAPTER 07 Modern AI

Large Language Models

What pretraining really is, the scaling laws that predict model quality, and the tricks used to decode text at inference.

7.1 Pretraining: Predict the Next Token

A large language model is, at its core, a transformer trained on one objective: given the previous tokens, predict the next one. That's it. No labels, no human supervision — the data supplies its own.

$$ L = -\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log P_\theta(x_t \mid x_{This single objective, applied to trillions of tokens of text, produces a model that can translate, summarize, code, explain jokes, and reason — because all of those behaviors appear implicitly in the training data.

Next-token prediction. Trained at planetary scale, this trivial objective produces emergent capability.

7.2 Scaling Laws

In 2020 OpenAI (Kaplan et al.) and later DeepMind (Chinchilla, 2022) showed that model loss follows smooth power-law relationships with compute, parameters, and data. Bigger and more data ⇒ predictably lower loss.

As you multiply compute by 10×, loss drops by a predictable fraction. Scaling laws are the reason labs spend billions on GPUs.

Chinchilla insight: for a fixed compute budget, you should roughly balance parameters and training tokens — about 20 tokens per parameter. Earlier models were over-parameterized and under-trained.

7.3 Mixture of Experts

A Mixture of Experts (MoE) layer replaces a single giant FFN with many smaller "expert" FFNs and a lightweight router that picks a small subset (typically 2 of 8 or 2 of 64) per token.

MoE: sparse activation. Only a few experts fire per token, so you can have huge models without linear cost.

7.4 Context Windows & KV Cache

The context window is how many tokens the model can "see" at once. It grew from 2K (GPT-2) → 8K (GPT-3.5) → 200K (Claude) → 1M+ (Gemini, Claude) in just a few years.

Self-attention cost is $O(T^2)$ in sequence length $T$, which is why long contexts are expensive. The KV cache makes inference efficient: for each token you generate, you only need to compute new queries, keys, and values — the previous K and V vectors are stored and reused.

7.5 Decoding Strategies

Given a probability distribution over the next token, how do you pick one? The choice shapes the output's character.

Greedy

Always pick the most likely token. Deterministic, boring, sometimes locally optimal but globally weird.

Temperature sampling

Divide logits by $T$. $T < 1$ sharpens toward greedy; $T > 1$ flattens toward uniform. Default in most chat APIs.

Top-k / Top-p (nucleus)

Truncate the distribution to only the top-k tokens, or to the smallest set whose probs sum to p. Kills rare-but-bad tokens.

Beam search

Keep multiple candidate prefixes, extend all of them. Great for translation; bad for open-ended generation (repetitive).

import torch, torch.nn.functional as F

@torch.no_grad()
def generate(model, input_ids, max_new_tokens=50, temperature=0.7, top_p=0.9):
    for _ in range(max_new_tokens):
        logits = model(input_ids)[:, -1]                 # last-token logits
        logits = logits / temperature

        # nucleus (top-p) filtering
        probs  = F.softmax(logits, dim=-1)
        sorted_probs, sorted_ix = probs.sort(descending=True)
        cum = sorted_probs.cumsum(dim=-1)
        mask = cum - sorted_probs > top_p
        sorted_probs[mask] = 0
        sorted_probs /= sorted_probs.sum(dim=-1, keepdim=True)

        next_tok = sorted_ix[0, torch.multinomial(sorted_probs[0], 1)]
        input_ids = torch.cat([input_ids, next_tok.view(1, 1)], dim=1)
    return input_ids
