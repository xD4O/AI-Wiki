Math Foundations

2.1 Linear Algebra

Neural networks are essentially stacks of matrix multiplications and simple non-linear functions. If you can do matrix math, you can read every AI paper.

Vectors and Matrices

A vector is an ordered list of numbers; a matrix is a rectangular grid. In ML, data points are usually vectors, and datasets are matrices.

$$ \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_n \end{bmatrix} \qquad \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} & \cdots & a_{1n} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ a_{m1} & \cdots & a_{mn} \end{bmatrix} $$
Matrix multiplication — the single most important operation in deep learning.

Key Operations

  • Dot product: $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \sum_i a_i b_i$ — measures similarity between two vectors.
  • Matrix-vector product: $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}$ — the core operation of a neural network layer.
  • Transpose: $\mathbf{A}^T$ — flip rows and columns.
  • Norm: $\|\mathbf{x}\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_i x_i^2}$ — the "length" of a vector.

2.2 Calculus & Gradients

Training a neural network means finding parameters that minimize a loss function. We do that by computing gradients — derivatives that point in the direction of steepest ascent — and walking in the opposite direction.

For a function $f(x)$, the derivative is:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} $$

For multi-variable functions, the gradient $\nabla f$ is the vector of partial derivatives:

$$ \nabla f(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1} \\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2} \\ \vdots \\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n} \end{bmatrix} $$
Gradient descent walks downhill on the loss surface, one step at a time.

The Chain Rule

The chain rule is the mathematical engine of backpropagation. If $y = f(g(x))$, then:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{dg} \cdot \frac{dg}{dx} $$

Composing functions composes their derivatives. That's why you can train a deep neural network: derivatives flow cleanly backward through every layer.

2.3 Probability

ML models produce distributions over possible answers, not single answers. Probability is how we reason about uncertainty.

ConceptFormulaMeaning
Conditional$P(A \mid B)$Probability of A given B happened
Joint$P(A, B)$Probability both A and B happen
Bayes' theorem$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A) P(A)}{P(B)}$Update beliefs from evidence
Expectation$\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x x \cdot P(x)$Long-run average
Variance$\text{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2]$Spread around the mean

The softmax function turns a vector of scores into a probability distribution — it's what language models use to pick the next token.

$$ \text{softmax}(z)_i = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}} $$

2.4 Information Theory

Claude Shannon's information theory gives us the loss functions that drive almost all of modern deep learning.

Entropy measures the uncertainty in a distribution:

$$ H(p) = -\sum_i p_i \log p_i $$

Cross-entropy measures how "surprised" distribution $p$ is by distribution $q$ — and it's the standard loss for classification:

$$ H(p, q) = -\sum_i p_i \log q_i $$

KL divergence measures how different two distributions are:

$$ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \| q) = \sum_i p_i \log \frac{p_i}{q_i} $$
Why cross-entropy is everywhere

When you train a classifier — including every LLM — minimizing cross-entropy is equivalent to maximum-likelihood estimation. You're asking: "what parameters make my model most likely to have generated the observed data?"

2.5 Doing It in NumPy

Every concept above is one line of NumPy.

import numpy as np

# ---- Linear algebra ----
A = np.array([[1, 2, 3], [4, 5, 6]])           # 2×3 matrix
B = np.array([[7, 8], [9, 10], [11, 12]])      # 3×2 matrix
C = A @ B                                       # matrix multiply → 2×2
print(C)
# [[ 58  64]
#  [139 154]]

x = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0])
print("norm:", np.linalg.norm(x))              # ||x||₂

# ---- Probability: softmax ----
def softmax(z):
    z = z - np.max(z)                           # numerical stability
    e = np.exp(z)
    return e / e.sum()

logits = np.array([2.0, 1.0, 0.1])
print("probs:", softmax(logits))                # [0.66 0.24 0.10]

# ---- Information theory: cross-entropy ----
def cross_entropy(p, q, eps=1e-12):
    return -np.sum(p * np.log(q + eps))

true  = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0])               # one-hot label
pred  = softmax(logits)
print("loss:", cross_entropy(true, pred))       # ≈ 0.42

# ---- Calculus: numerical gradient check ----
def f(x):
    return (x ** 2).sum()

def num_grad(f, x, h=1e-5):
    g = np.zeros_like(x)
    for i in range(len(x)):
        xp, xm = x.copy(), x.copy()
        xp[i] += h; xm[i] -= h
        g[i] = (f(xp) - f(xm)) / (2 * h)
    return g

x = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0])
print("∇f:", num_grad(f, x))                    # [2, 4, 6]  (analytical: 2x)
